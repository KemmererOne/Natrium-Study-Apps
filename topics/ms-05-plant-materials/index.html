Properties that drive material selection

Objectives 1.1, 1.2

Selection covers both non-fuel materials, used for structures and components, and fuel materials. The properties below are the ones the handbook lists for nuclear facility construction. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.1

Machinability describes how a metal reacts to mechanical deformation by removing chips, with respect to the amount of metal effectively removed and the surface finish attainable. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.1

"Many components used in nuclear reactor construction use machined parts that require very close tolerances and very smooth surfaces." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.1

Formability is a material's ability to withstand peripheral expansion without failure, or its capacity to be manufactured into the final required shape. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.2

"This becomes important in selecting materials that have to be made into specific shapes by such means as rolling or bending and still retain their required strength." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.2
"Stability of a material refers to its mechanical and chemical inertness under the conditions to which it will be subjected." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.2
"High corrosion resistance is desirable in reactor systems because low corrosion resistance leads to increased production of corrosion products that may be transported through the core." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.2

Those corrosion products become irradiated and contaminate the system, which contributes to high radiation levels after shutdown. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.2

"Fabricability is a measure of the ease with which a material can be worked and made into desirable shapes and forms." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.3
"Good heat transfer properties are desirable from the fuel boundary to the coolant in order that the heat produced will be efficiently transferred." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.3
"The availability of a material used in the construction of nuclear plants refers to the ease with which a material can be obtained and its cost." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.2
  • Ductility: important for materials under tension and compression, and essential for reactor vessel steels
  • Heat transfer: most important for core cladding and heat exchanger tubes
  • Mechanical strength: desirable because of possible degradation from radiation damage and the need to contain radioactive liquids and fuel
  • Cost: a major part of facility capital cost is plant material

DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.3

Fast-spectrum fuel materials

Objectives 1.3

Objective 1.3 asks for the materials that fission by thermal neutrons. That list is thermal-spectrum content, so it stays off this page under scope Rule 8, and the objective is marked for review. The handbook's fast-spectrum statements are below. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.5

"The isotopes uranium-238 (238U) and thorium-232 (232Th) are fissionable by fast neutrons." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.5
"Mixed oxides of uranium and plutonium are used in liquid-metal fast breeder reactors (LMFBR)." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.6

Plutonium dioxide is not used alone as a reactor fuel. It is mixed with uranium dioxide, at about 20% plutonium dioxide for fast reactor fuel. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.6

The use of plutonium-239 in fast reactors is economical because breeding takes place, which produces more plutonium-239 than fission consumes. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.6

Uranium dioxide pellets are clad with stainless steel in fast sodium-cooled reactors to form fuel elements. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.6

"The cladding protects the fuel from attack by the coolant, prevents the escape of fission products, and provides geometrical integrity." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.6
"Under the much more severe conditions in a fast reactor, however, even inert UO2 begins to respond to its environment in a manner that is often detrimental to fuel performance." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.6

At Natrium, the PSAR says: PSAR Rev 1, ML25276A288, Sec. 7.1.1.1, p. 7.1-1

"The initial fuel for the core is sodium-bonded uranium - 10 weight percent Zirconium (U-10Zr) that is encapsulated by HT-9 ferritic-martensitic steel cladding." PSAR Rev 1, ML25276A288, Sec. 7.1.1.1, p. 7.1-1

Selecting a fuel material

Objectives 1.4

"The nuclear properties of a material must be the first consideration in the selection of a suitable nuclear fuel." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.8

Those nuclear properties bear on neutron economy: absorption and fission cross sections, the reactions and products that result, neutron production, and the energy released. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.8

  • High thermal conductivity, so high power can be reached without excessive fuel temperature gradients
  • Resistance to radiation damage, so physical properties are not degraded
  • Chemical stability with respect to coolant in case of cladding failure
  • Physical and mechanical properties that permit economical fabrication

DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.10

"High thermal conductivity allows high power density and high specific power without excessive fuel temperature gradients." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.9
"The dimensional stability of the fuel under conditions of high temperature and high burnup is of primary importance in determining the usable lifetime." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.9

Swelling compromises dimensional stability. It constricts the coolant channels and may lead to rupture of the metal cladding and escape of fission products into the coolant. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.9

"The main cause of fuel element swelling is the accumulation of two fission product atoms for each atom fissioned." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.9

Some of the fission products are gases, so a ceramic fuel's ability to retain and accommodate fission gases is of primary importance to core lifetime. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.9

Fuel cladding

Objectives 1.5, 1.6

"Cladding is used to provide surface protection for retaining fission products and minimizing corrosion." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.11

Cladding also contains pelletized fuel in the required physical configuration. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.11

"Mechanical properties, such as ductility, impact strength, tensile strength, and creep, must be adequate for the operating conditions of the reactor core." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.11
"The cladding must have a high corrosion resistance to its operating environment." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.11

Shielding materials

Objectives 1.11

Shield design depends on the location, intensity, and energy distribution of the radiation sources, and on the permissible radiation levels away from them. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.18

"At low energies (less than 0.1 MeV), low mass number materials, such as hydrogen in H2O, are best for slowing down neutrons." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.18

Materials containing hydrogen are called hydrogenous material, and their value as a neutron shield is set by their hydrogen content. Water ranks high as a neutron shield but is a poor absorber of gamma radiation. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.18

"To offset this decrease in cross section with increased neutron energy, materials with good inelastic scattering properties, such as iron, are used." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.19

These materials cause a large change in neutron energy after collision for high energy neutrons, but have little effect on neutrons below 0.1 MeV. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.19

"Gamma radiation is the most difficult to shield against and, therefore, presents the biggest problem in the reactor plant." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.19
"Gamma shielding is therefore more effectively performed by materials with high atomic mass number and high density." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.20
"Because of its low melting point, lead can be used only where the temperatures do not exceed its melting point." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.20

Iron also works well as a gamma attenuator, and it is often chosen for structural, temperature, and economic reasons. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.20

"As a general shield material, there is much to recommend about concrete; it is strong, inexpensive, and adaptable to both block and monolithic types of construction." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.20
"The beta particle can generally be stopped by a sheet of aluminum." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.20

All materials described for neutron and gamma radiation also attenuate beta radiation. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.20

"Since alpha and beta particles can be easily shielded against, they do not present a major problem in the nuclear reactor plant." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.21

Pellet-cladding interaction and fission product swelling

Objectives 1.12, 1.13

"Fuel pellet-cladding interaction (PCI) may lead to cladding failure and subsequent release of fission products into the reactor coolant." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.22
"Expansion of the fuel pellets due to high internal temperatures, cracking due to thermal stresses, and irradiation-induced swelling may lead to contact of the fuel with the cladding." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.22

PCI tends to occur under power ramping conditions, and it appears more likely during initial power increase. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.22

  • An increase in the cladding thickness
  • An increase in the cladding-pellet gap, with pressurization to prevent cladding collapse
  • A layer of graphite or other lubricant between the fuel and the cladding

DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.22

"Operational limitations such as rate of power increase and power for a given power ramp rate are imposed to lessen the effect of PCI." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.23

Swelling of oxide fuels is attributed to gaseous fission-product bubble formation and the accumulation of solid fission products. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.25

"Swelling can cause excessive pressure on the cladding, which could lead to fuel element cladding failure." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.25

Fuel element life, or core burnup, is also set by the decrease in reactivity from the loss of fissile material and the accumulation of fission-product poisons. Operational maximum and minimum coolant flow limits help prevent extensive fuel element damage. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.25

Fatigue, work hardening, and creep

Objectives 1.14, 1.15

"Fatigue failure is defined as the tendency of a material to fracture by means of progressive brittle cracking under repeated alternating or cyclic stresses of an intensity considerably below the normal strength." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.27

There is very little, if any, warning before failure if the crack is not noticed. The number of cycles to failure at a given peak stress is generally large, but it decreases as the stress increases. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.27

"Thermal fatigue can arise from thermal stresses produced by cyclic changes in temperature." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.27

The reactor vessel and piping see cyclic stresses from temperature variations during startup, power changes, and shutdown. Because they see large load variations at a low cycle frequency, high ductility is the main requirement for their steel. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.27

Fatigue can start at microscopic cracks and notches, and even at grinding and machining marks, so such defects must be avoided in materials under cyclic stress. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.28

"Heatup and cooldown limitations, pressure limitations, and pump operating curves are all used to minimize cyclic stress." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.28
"In some cases, cycle logs may be kept on various pieces of equipment." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.28

Work hardening occurs when a metal is strained beyond the yield point. An increasing stress is then needed for more plastic deformation, and the metal apparently becomes stronger and harder to deform. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.28

"Work hardening reduces ductility, which increases the chances of brittle failure." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.28

Prior work hardening, or cold working, gives the treated material an apparently higher yield stress, so the metal is strengthened. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.29

"At elevated temperatures and constant stress or load, many materials continue to deform at a slow rate." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.29

That behavior is creep. Stage I is primary or transient creep, where the rate starts high and soon decreases. Stage II is secondary or steady-state creep, with a small rate. In Stage III, tertiary or accelerating creep, the rate increases and the strain may result in failure. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.29

The creep rate depends strongly on both stress and temperature. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.30

"For safe operation, the total deformation due to creep must be well below the strain at which failure occurs." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.30
"Similarly, creep is a consideration at the temperatures that stainless-steel cladding encounters in gas-cooled reactors and fast reactors" DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.30

Radiation interactions with metals

Objectives 1.16, 1.17

Beta and gamma radiation produce ionization and excitation of electrons in metals, which does very little damage because electrons are relatively free to move and are soon replaced. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.32

"The net effect of beta and gamma radiation on metal is to generate a small amount of heat." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.32
"Heavier particles, such as protons, α-particles, fast neutrons, and fission fragments, will usually transfer sufficient energy through elastic or inelastic collisions to remove nuclei from their lattice (crystalline) positions." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.32

A struck nucleus that gains about 25 eV of kinetic energy, 25 eV to 30 eV for most metals, is displaced from its equilibrium position in the crystal lattice. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.33

  • Knock-on: the displaced target nucleus, or recoiling atom
  • Vacancy: the site vacated when a metal atom is ejected from its crystal lattice
  • Interstitial: a permanently displaced atom that has lost its energy and sits at a position other than a normal lattice site

DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.34

"A single fast neutron in the greater than or equal to 1 MeV range can displace a few thousand atoms." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.33

Most of those displacements are temporary. Over a long irradiation many displaced atoms return to normal lattice sites. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.34

"The presence of interstitials and vacancies makes it more difficult for dislocations to move through the lattice." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.34
"This increases the strength and reduces the ductility of a material." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.34

A thermal spike occurs when radiation deposits energy as a knock-on, which transfers its excess energy to the surrounding atoms as vibrational energy, or heat. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.35

Fast neutron effects on metal properties

Objectives 1.0

"The general physical and mechanical effects of the irradiation of metals by fast neutrons and other high-energy particles are summarized in Table 1." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.39
  • Properties that increase: yield strength, tensile strength, NDT temperature, Young's modulus (slightly), hardness, and high-temperature creep rate during irradiation
  • Properties that decrease: ductility, stress-rupture strength, density, impact strength, and thermal conductivity

DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.39

The effects are generally less significant at elevated temperatures for a given fluence, and some defects can be removed by heating, or annealing. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.39

"The increase in the NDT temperature is one of the most important effects of irradiation from the standpoint of nuclear power system design." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.40

A rising NDT temperature with lower ductility increases the chance of brittle fracture. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.40

Irradiation often decreases the density of a metal over a certain temperature range, so a specimen swells. This swelling in stainless steel structures and fuel rod cladding is a matter of great concern in fast reactors. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.41

"The swelling can cause changes in the dimensions of the coolant channels and also interfere with the free movement of control elements." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.41

When irradiation-induced swelling occurs, it is usually significant only from roughly 0.3 Tm to 0.5 Tm, where Tm is the melting point of the metal in kelvin. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.42

"In stainless steel, trace amounts of boron undergo a (n,α) reaction that generates helium bubbles which lead to the deterioration of mechanical properties." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.39

Helium bubbles can also enhance embrittlement. The accepted view is stress-induced bubble growth at the grain boundaries, where bubbles link up and cause intergranular failure. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.43

Radiation effects in organic compounds

Objectives 1.23, 1.24, 1.25, 1.26

Gamma and beta radiation cause very little damage in metals, but they break the chemical bonds of organic compounds, prevent bond recombination, and cause permanent damage. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.45

"Ionization is the major damage mechanism in organic compounds." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.45

Materials sensitive to ionization include insulators, dielectrics, plastics, lubricants, hydraulic fluids, and rubber. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.45

"Nylon begins to suffer degradation of its toughness at relatively low doses, but suffers little loss in strength." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.45
"High-density (linear) polyethylene marlex 50 loses both strength and ductility at relatively low doses." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.46
"In general, rubber will harden upon being irradiated." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.46

Butyl or Thiokol rubber, however, will soften or become liquid with high radiation doses. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.46

"It is important that oils and greases be evaluated for their resistance to radiation if they are to be employed in a high-radiation environment." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.46

The chemical bond with the least resistance to decomposition is the covalent bond, in which outer electrons are shared by two atoms rather than firmly attached to one. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.46

"This recombination of broken hydrocarbon chains into longer ones is called" DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.46

That word is polymerization. It is one of the reactions that takes place in organic compounds during irradiation, and it changes their properties. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-05 p.46

Objective 1.20Needs sodium review

Recoil energy after a capture gamma

An atom of mass number 10 emits a 7 MeV gamma ray after capturing a neutron. Find the recoil energy and compare it with the energy needed to displace an atom.

  1. The handbook gives Er = 5.4 x 10^-4 x Eγ² / A, with energies in MeV and A in atomic mass units.
  2. Er = 5.4 x 10^-4 x (7)² / 10 = 5.4 x 10^-4 x 4.9 = 2.6 x 10^-3 MeV.
  3. That is about 2.6 keV, or 2600 eV.

Answer: About 2.6 keV, far above the roughly 25 eV needed to displace an atom. This agrees with the handbook's 2 keV to 3 keV for an element of low atomic mass.
