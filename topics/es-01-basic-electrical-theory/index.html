Charges and electrostatic force

Objectives 1.1

An atom consists of a positively charged nucleus surrounded by negatively charged electrons, so the atom as a whole is electrically neutral. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.1

"This attraction is called electrostatic force, the force that holds the electron in orbit." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.2
"unlike charges (like electrons and protons) attract each other, and like charges repel each other." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.3

These facts are known as the First Law of Electrostatics. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.3

"Forces of this type are the result of an electrostatic field that exists around each charged particle or object." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.3

The strength of the attraction or repulsion depends on the amount of charge on each object and the distance between them. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.5

"Potential difference is the term used to describe how large the electrostatic force is between two charged objects." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.5

The basic unit of measure of potential difference is the volt, so potential difference is also called voltage. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.6

"The sum of the potential differences of all charged particles in the electrostatic field is referred to as electromotive force (EMF)." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.6

The electrons in the outermost shell are called valence electrons. Enough applied energy makes some of them leave the atom, and these are free electrons. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.7

"If the atom loses one or more electrons, it becomes positively charged and is referred to as a positive ion." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.7

If an atom gains one or more electrons, it becomes negatively charged and is referred to as a negative ion. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.7

Electrical terminology

Objectives 1.2

"Conductors are materials with electrons that are loosely bound to their atoms, or materials that permit free motion of a large number of electrons." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.9

Copper, silver, and gold are examples of good conductors. Most metals are good conductors. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.9

"Insulators, or nonconductors, are materials with electrons that are tightly bound to their atoms and require large amounts of energy to free them from the influence of the nucleus." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.9

Examples of insulators are rubber, plastics, glass, and dry wood. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.9

"Resistors are made of materials that conduct electricity, but offer opposition to current flow." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.9

Electron flow is from a point of negative potential to a point of positive potential. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.11

"This flow of positive charges is known as conventional current" DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.11

Both conventions are in use and they are essentially equivalent. This handbook uses electron flow. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.11

"A direct current flows continuously in the same direction." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.12
"An alternating current periodically reverses direction." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.12
"An ideal source is a theoretical concept of an electric current or voltage supply (such as a battery) that has no losses and is a perfect voltage or current supply." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.12

Ideal sources are used for analytical purposes only, since they cannot occur in nature. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.12

"A real source is a real life current or voltage supply that has some losses associated with it." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.12

Electrical units and Ohm's Law

Objectives 1.3, 1.4

Voltage is the pressure or force that causes electrons to move in a conductor. It is measured in volts and written as E in equations. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.13

Current is the movement of free electrons through a conductor. It is measured in amperes and written as I. One ampere is one coulomb of charge moving past a given point in one second. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.10

"Resistance is defined as the opposition to current flow." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.14

Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω) and written as R. One ohm limits the current to one ampere when one volt is applied. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.14

"Applied voltage equals circuit current times the circuit resistance." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.14

E = IR DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.18

"If any two of the component values are known, the third can be calculated." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.15
"Conductance is found by taking the reciprocal of the resistance." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.16

G = 1/R DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.18

The unit of conductance is the mho, which is ohm spelled backwards. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.16

"power is the rate at which work is done, or the rate at which heat is generated." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.16

Electric power is measured in watts. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.16

P = I x E or P = I²R DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.18

"Inductance is defined as the ability of a coil to store energy, induce a voltage in itself, and oppose changes in current flowing through it." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.17

Inductance (L) is measured in henries. One henry permits one volt to be induced when the current through the coil changes at one ampere per second. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.17

"Capacitance is defined as the ability to store an electric charge" DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.17

Capacitance (C) is measured in farads. It equals the charge stored divided by the voltage applied. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.17

Objective 1.3Q621

In what unit is capacitance measured?

The answer is B. Capacitance is measured in farads. Henries measure inductance, mhos measure conductance, and webers measure magnetic flux. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.17

Objective 1.3Q622

A 4 ohm resistor has what conductance?

The answer is A. Conductance is the reciprocal of resistance: G = 1/R = 1/4 = 0.25 mho. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.16

Objective 1.3Q623

A current of 2 amps flows through 10 ohms. Using P = I²R, what is the power?

The answer is C. P = I²R = (2)²(10) = 40 watts. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.17

A circuit has 24 volts applied across 6 ohms. What current flows?

The answer is B. By Ohm's Law, I = E/R = 24/6 = 4 amps. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.14

Methods of producing voltage

Objectives 1.5

Electrochemistry: a chemical reaction produces and maintains opposite charges on two dissimilar metals in contact with an electrolyte solution, as in the voltaic chemical cell. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.19

Static electricity: removing electrons from a body, as happens due to friction, leaves it with an unequal charge, and an electric force is exerted between it and a body with a normal charge. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.20

"A generator is a machine that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy by using the principle of magnetic induction." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.21

Magnetic induction produces a voltage by rotating coils of wire through a stationary magnetic field, or by rotating a magnetic field through stationary coils of wire. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.21

Pressure on certain crystals or ceramics drives electrons out of orbit in the direction of the force, building up opposite charges on opposite sides. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.21

"This generation of voltage is known as the piezoelectric effect." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.21

When two dissimilar metals are joined and heat is applied to the junction, electrons transfer between them and a voltage appears across the junction. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.22

"This process is called thermoelectricity." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.22

When photons in a light beam strike a material, the energy transfer may dislodge electrons from their orbits, leaving the material positively charged. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.23

"This phenomenon is called the photoelectric effect" DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.24

At sufficiently high temperatures, a considerable number of electrons are able to escape from a hot cathode. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.24

"The liberation of electrons from a hot surface is called thermionic emission." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.24

Magnetism

Objectives 1.6, 1.7

The law of magnetism states that like magnetic poles repel and unlike magnetic poles attract one another. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.28

"The group of magnetic field lines emitted outward from the north pole of a magnet is called magnetic flux." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.29

The SI unit of magnetic flux is the weber (Wb). One weber is equal to 1 x 10⁸ magnetic field lines. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.29

"Magnetic flux density is the amount of magnetic flux per unit area of a section, perpendicular to the direction of flux." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.29

B = Φ / A DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.29

B is flux density in teslas, Φ is flux in webers, and A is area in square meters. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.29

"Permeability (µ) refers to the ability of a material to concentrate magnetic lines of flux." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.30

Relative permeability is the ratio of the permeability of a material to the permeability of a vacuum. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.30

Ferromagnetic materials include iron, steel, nickel, cobalt, and the commercial alloys alnico and peralloy. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.30

Paramagnetic materials include aluminum, platinum, manganese, and chromium. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.30

"These materials have a relative permeability of slightly more than one." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.30

Diamagnetic materials include bismuth, antimony, copper, zinc, mercury, gold, and silver. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.30

"These materials have a relative permeability of less than one." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.30

An electric current flowing through a conductor produces a magnetic field around that conductor. The left-hand rule relates the current flow to the direction of the magnetic lines of force. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.31

"Magnetomotive force (mmf) is the strength of a magnetic field in a coil of wire." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.33

Fm = ampere-turns = NI DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.33

Field intensity depends on the length of the coil: H = NI / L, in ampere-turns per meter. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.34

"Opposition to the production of flux in a material is called reluctance, which corresponds to resistance." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.35

Reluctance is inversely proportional to permeability. Iron cores have high permeability and low reluctance. Air has low permeability and high reluctance. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.35

A coil has 200 turns and carries 3 amps. What is its magnetomotive force?

Magnetic circuits and hysteresis

Objectives 1.8, 1.9, 1.10, 1.11

A magnetic circuit can be compared with an electric circuit. The mmf can be compared with EMF, and the flux can be compared to current. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.37

Φ = Fm / R DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.37

Reluctance is found from the length of the coil, the permeability of the magnetic material, and the cross-sectional area of the coil. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.38

"The BH Magnetization Curve (Figure 27) shows how much flux density (B) results from increasing the flux intensity (H)." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.39

Flux density increases rapidly with flux intensity before the core saturates, or develops a knee. After that, an increase in flux intensity has little or no effect on flux density. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.39

When current in a coil reverses direction thousands of times per second, hysteresis can cause considerable loss of energy. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.40

"The magnetic flux in an iron core lags behind the magnetizing force." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.40

Retentivity is the flux density remaining after the magnetizing force is zero. Coercive force is the force that must be applied in the reverse direction to reduce flux density to zero. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.41

"The greater the area inside the hysteresis loop, the larger the hysteresis losses." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.41

Induced voltage

Objectives 1.12, 1.13

If a conductor cuts across lines of magnetic force, or if magnetic lines of force cut across a conductor, a voltage is induced into the conductor. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.41

"there must be relative motion between the conductor and the magnetic lines of force in order to induce an EMF." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.42
"The magnitude of the induced voltage depends on two factors: (1) the number of turns of a coil, and (2) how fast the conductor cuts across the magnetic lines of force, or flux." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.42

Vind = -N (ΔΦ / Δt) DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.42

N is the number of turns in the coil, and ΔΦ/Δt is the rate at which the flux cuts across the conductor. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.43

"Induced voltage has a polarity that will oppose the change causing the induction." DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.43

The negative sign in the equation shows that the induced voltage acts to oppose the change. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.43

Objective 1.4

Finding resistance

Given that I = 2 A and E = 12 V, find the circuit resistance.

  1. R = E / I.
  2. R = 12 V / 2 A.

Answer: 6 Ω

DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.15

Objective 1.4

Finding voltage

Find the applied voltage for a circuit resistance of 100 Ω and a circuit current of 0.5 amps.

  1. E = IR.
  2. E = (0.5 A)(100 Ω).

Answer: 50 V

DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.16

Objective 1.6

Ampere-turns

Calculate the ampere-turns for a coil with 1000 turns and a 5 mA current.

  1. N = 1000 turns and I = 5 x 10⁻³ A.
  2. NI = 1000 (5 x 10⁻³).

Answer: 5 At

DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.34

Objective 1.9

Flux in a magnetic circuit

A coil has an mmf of 600 At and a reluctance of 3 x 10⁶ At/Wb. Find the total flux.

  1. Φ = Fm / R.
  2. Φ = 600 At / (3 x 10⁶ At/Wb).

Answer: 200 µWb

DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.39

Objective 1.12

Induced voltage

Flux increases uniformly from 4 Wb to 8 Wb in 2 seconds. Find the induced voltage in a stationary coil with 12 turns.

  1. ΔΦ = 8 Wb - 4 Wb = 4 Wb, and Δt = 2 s.
  2. ΔΦ/Δt = 4 Wb / 2 s = 2 Wb/s.
  3. Vind = -12 (2).

Answer: -24 volts

Summary

  • Electrostatic force holds an electron in orbit around a nucleus, and potential difference measures how large that force is between two charged objects. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.8
  • Conductors have electrons loosely bound to their atoms, and insulators have electrons tightly bound to their atoms. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.12
  • Voltage is measured in volts, resistance in ohms, conductance in mhos, power in watts, inductance in henries, and capacitance in farads. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.18
  • Voltage can be produced by electrochemistry, static electricity, magnetic induction, the piezoelectric effect, thermoelectricity, the photoelectric effect, and thermionic emission. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.26
  • Reluctance is the opposition to the production of flux in a material. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.36
  • A BH curve shows how much flux density results from increasing flux intensity, and the knee marks where further increases have minimal effect. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.44
  • Faraday's Law: induced voltage depends on the number of turns of a coil and how fast the conductor cuts the flux. Lenz's Law: an induced voltage opposes the change that caused it. DOE-HDBK-1011/1-92 ES-01 p.44
