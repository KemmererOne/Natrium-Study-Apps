Bonding

Objectives 1.1

"Solid matter is held together by forces originating between neighboring atoms or molecules." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.1

These forces arise because of differences in the electron clouds of atoms. The valence electrons, those in the outer shell, determine an atom's attraction for its neighbors. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.1

"Ionic bond - In this type of bond, one or more electrons are wholly transferred from an atom of one element to the atom of the other, and the elements are held together by the force of attraction due to the opposite polarity of the charge." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.1
"Covalent bond - A bond formed by shared electrons." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.1

Electrons are shared when an atom needs electrons to complete its outer shell and can share them with its neighbor. The electrons are then part of both atoms and both shells are filled. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.1

"Metallic bond - In this type of bond, the atoms do not share or exchange electrons to bond together." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.2
"Instead, many electrons (roughly one for each atom) are more or less free to move throughout the metal, so that each electron can interact with many of the fixed atoms." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.2

In a molecular bond, the electrons of neutral atoms spend more time in one region of their orbit, so a temporary weak charge exists and the molecule weakly attracts other molecules. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.2

"This is sometimes called the van der Waals or molecular bonds." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.2
"Hydrogen bond - This bond is similar to the molecular bond and occurs due to the ease with which hydrogen atoms are willing to give up an electron to atoms of oxygen, fluorine, or nitrogen." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.2
  • Sodium chloride: ionic
  • Diamond: covalent
  • Sodium: metallic
  • Solid H2: molecular
  • Ice: hydrogen

DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.2

Properties such as the ability to conduct heat or electrical current are set by the freedom of movement of electrons, which depends on the type of bonding. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.2

Amorphous and crystalline solids

Objectives 1.2

The properties of materials depend on their interatomic bonds. All solids are either amorphous or crystalline. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.4

"Amorphous materials have no regular arrangement of their molecules." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.4

Glass and paraffin are amorphous. Amorphous materials have definite shape and volume, diffuse slowly, and lack sharply defined melting points. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.4

"In a crystalline structure, the atoms are arranged in a three-dimensional array called a lattice." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.4

The lattice has a regular repeating configuration in all directions. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.4

Common lattice types

Objectives 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, 1.5

"A crystal structure consists of atoms arranged in a pattern that repeats periodically in a three-dimensional geometric lattice." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.6

This repeated pattern controls properties like strength, ductility, density, conductivity, and shape. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.6

"In a body-centered cubic (BCC) arrangement of atoms, the unit cell consists of eight atoms at the corners of a cube and one atom at the body center of the cube." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.6
"In a face-centered cubic (FCC) arrangement of atoms, the unit cell consists of eight atoms at the corners of a cube and one atom at the center of each of the faces of the cube." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.7
"In a hexagonal close-packed (HCP) arrangement of atoms, the unit cell consists of three layers of atoms." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.7

The top and bottom layers contain six atoms at the corners of a hexagon and one atom at the center of each hexagon. The middle layer contains three atoms nestled between the atoms of the top and bottom layers. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.7

"Metals containing BCC structures include ferrite, chromium, vanadium, molybdenum, and tungsten." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.8

BCC metals have high strength and low ductility. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.8

"Metals containing FCC structures include austenite, aluminum, copper, lead, silver, gold, nickel, platinum, and thorium." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.8

FCC metals are, in general, of lower strength and higher ductility than BCC metals. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.8

"Metals containing HCP structures include beryllium, magnesium, zinc, cadmium, cobalt, thallium, and zirconium." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.8

HCP metals are not as ductile as FCC metals. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.8

Grains, grain boundaries, and creep

Objectives 1.6

"Grain is the region of space occupied by a continuous crystal lattice." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.11
"Grain structure is the arrangement of grains in a metal, with a grain having a particular crystal structure." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.11
"Grain boundary is the outside area of grain that separates it from other grains." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.11
"The grain boundary is a region of misfit between the grains and is usually one to three atom diameters wide." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.9
"For example, smaller grain size increases tensile strength and tends to increase ductility." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.9
"A larger grain size is preferred for improved high-temperature creep properties." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.9
"Creep is the permanent deformation that increases with time under constant load or stress." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.9

Creep becomes progressively easier with increasing temperature. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.9

Cross rolling can give a random grain orientation. Rolling enough in one direction can give a grain-oriented structure, called preferred orientation. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.10

"For example, preferred orientation in uranium fuel elements can result in catastrophic changes in dimensions during use in a nuclear reactor." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.10

Polymorphism and uranium

Objectives 1.7, 1.8, 1.9

"Polymorphism is the property or ability of a metal to exist in two or more crystalline forms depending upon temperature and composition." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.14

Most metals and metal alloys exhibit this property. Uranium metal can exist in three different crystalline structures. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.12

  • Alpha phase: room temperature to 663°C
  • Beta phase: 663°C to 764°C
  • Gamma phase: 764°C to its melting point of 1133°C

DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.12

The alpha phase is stable at room temperature. Its lattice properties differ in the X, Y, and Z axes, so when heated it expands in the X and Z directions and shrinks in the Y direction. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.13

"As shown, heating and cooling of alpha phase uranium can lead to drastic dimensional changes and gross distortions of the metal." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.13
"Thus, pure uranium is not used as a fuel, but only in alloys or compounds." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.13
"Alpha phase prevents pure uranium from being used as fuel because of expansion properties." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.14

The beta phase has a tetragonal lattice. The gamma phase is BCC and expands equally in all directions when heated. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.13

Iron also shows polymorphism: heating to 907°C changes it from BCC (alpha, ferrite) to FCC (gamma, austenite). Zirconium is HCP (alpha) up to 863°C, where it becomes BCC. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.14

Alloys

Objectives 1.10, 1.11, 1.12

"An alloy is a mixture of two or more materials, at least one of which is a metal." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.15
  • Solid solutions, where secondary atoms are introduced as substitutionals or interstitials in a crystal lattice
  • A crystal with a metallic compound at each lattice point
  • Composites, where secondary crystals are imbedded in a primary polycrystalline matrix

DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.15

"Alloys are usually stronger than pure metals, although they generally offer reduced electrical and thermal conductivity." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.15

Steel is an alloy of iron and carbon, with other elements to give it certain desirable properties. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.15

"The two desirable properties of type 304 stainless steel are corrosion resistance and high toughness." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.17

Type 304 stainless steel contains 18%-20% chromium and 8%-10.5% nickel. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.16

"Stainless steels are classified as austenitic or ferritic based on their lattice structure." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.16

Austenitic stainless steels, including 304 and 316, have a face-centered cubic structure. Ferritic stainless steels, including type 405, have a body-centered cubic lattice and contain no nickel. Ferritic steels are easier to weld and fabricate and less susceptible to stress corrosion cracking, but have only moderate resistance to other chemical attack. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.16

Microscopic imperfections

Objectives 1.13, 1.14

"Microscopic imperfections are generally classified as either point, line, or interfacial imperfections." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.18
  • Point imperfections have atomic dimensions.
  • Line imperfections, or dislocations, are generally many atoms in length.
  • Interfacial imperfections are larger than line defects and occur over a two-dimensional area.

DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.18

"Vacancy defects result from a missing atom in a lattice position." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.18
"Substitutional defects result from an impurity present at a lattice position." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.18

Interstitial defects come from an impurity at an interstitial site, or a lattice atom in an interstitial position. Interstitial refers to locations between atoms in a lattice. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.18

Line imperfections occur in crystalline materials only, and can be edge, screw, or mixed type. They cannot end inside a crystal. They must end at a crystal edge or another dislocation, or close back on themselves. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.19

"Slip occurs when a crystal is subjected to stress and the dislocations march through the crystal until they reach the edge or are arrested by another dislocation." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.22

The slip of one active plane is ordinarily about 1000 atomic distances, and yielding requires slip on many planes. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.20

Interfacial imperfections are found at free surfaces, domain boundaries, grain boundaries, or interphase boundaries. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.21

Bulk defects

Objectives 1.15

"Three-dimensional macroscopic defects are called bulk defects." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.21

They occur on a much larger scale than microscopic defects, and are generally introduced during refinement from the raw state or during fabrication. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.21

"The most common bulk defect arises from foreign particles being included in the prime material." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.21
  • Foreign particles included in the prime material (inclusions)
  • Gas pockets
  • Shrinking cavities
  • Welding or joining defects

DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.22

Working and forging can also cause cracks that act as stress concentrators and weaken the material. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.21

Summary

  • The five bond types are ionic, covalent, metallic, molecular, and hydrogen. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.5
  • A crystal structure consists of atoms arranged in a pattern that repeats periodically in a three-dimensional lattice, commonly BCC, FCC, or HCP. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.8
  • Grain is the region of space occupied by a continuous crystal lattice, and small grain size increases tensile strength and ductility. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.11
  • Alpha phase prevents pure uranium from being used as fuel because of expansion properties. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.14
  • Alloys are usually stronger than pure metals, with reduced electrical and thermal conductivities. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.17
  • Microscopic imperfections are point, line, and interfacial, and bulk defects are three dimensional. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-01 p.22
