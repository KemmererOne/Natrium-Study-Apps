Thermal shock

Objectives 1.1, 1.2

"Thermal shock (stress) can lead to excessive thermal gradients on materials, which lead to excessive stresses." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.1

These stresses can be tensile, from forces tending to pull a material apart, and compressive, from forces tending to push a material together. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.1

"These stresses, cyclic in nature, can lead to fatigue failure of the materials." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.1
"Thermal shock is caused by nonuniform heating or cooling of a uniform material, or uniform heating of nonuniform materials." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.1
"When the temperature of the material increases, the increased activity of the molecules causes them to press against the constraining boundaries, thus setting up thermal stresses." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.1

Calculating thermal stress

Objectives 1.3

If a material is not constrained, it expands. The thermal expansion coefficient α relates the fractional change in length, called thermal strain, to the change in temperature. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.2

Δl/l = αΔT DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.2

  • Carbon steel: 5.8 x 10⁻⁶ per °F
  • Stainless steel: 9.6 x 10⁻⁶ per °F
  • Aluminum: 13.3 x 10⁻⁶ per °F
  • Copper: 9.3 x 10⁻⁶ per °F
  • Lead: 16.3 x 10⁻⁶ per °F

DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.2

"In the simple case where two ends of a material are strictly constrained, the thermal stress can be calculated using Hooke's Law" DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.3

F/A = EαΔT, where F/A is thermal stress, E the modulus of elasticity, α the linear thermal expansion coefficient, and ΔT the change in temperature. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.3

Knowledge check: Calculating thermal stress

Objective 1.3Q1331

A carbon steel bar constrained at both ends is heated 100°F. E is 3.0 x 10⁷ psi and α is 5.8 x 10⁻⁶ per °F. What is the thermal stress?

The answer is B. F/A = EαΔT = (3.0 x 10⁷)(5.8 x 10⁻⁶)(100) = 17,400 psi. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.3

Report a problem
Objective 1.3Q1332

Which equation gives the thermal stress in a material constrained at both ends?

The answer is D. Equating strain from thermal expansion and from Hooke's Law gives F/A = EαΔT. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.3

Thick-walled vessels and operating limits

Objectives 1.4, 1.5

"Thermal stress is a major concern due to the magnitude of the stresses involved with rapid heating (or cooling)." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.5

With rapid heating or cooling of a thick-walled vessel, one part of the wall may try to expand or contract while the adjacent section, not yet at the new temperature, tries to restrain it. Both sections are under stress. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.4

If the wall is less than about 1 percent of the vessel's radius, it is usually considered thin-walled. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.4

"If the thickness of the vessel wall is more than 5 percent to 10 percent of the vessel's radius, it is considered a thick-walled vessel." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.4
"When the metal on the inside wall cools, it contracts, while the hot metal on the outside wall is still expanded." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.4
"This sets up a thermal stress, placing the cold side in tensile stress and the hot side in compressive stress, which can cause cracks in the cold side of the wall." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.4

Slow controlled heating and cooling of the reactor system are necessary to minimize cyclic thermal stress and the potential for fatigue failure. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.4

"Operating procedures are designed to reduce both the magnitude and the frequency of these stresses." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.4
  • Heatup and cooldown rate limits
  • Temperature limits for placing systems into operation
  • Specific temperatures for specific pressures for system operation

DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.5

"These limitations permit material structures to change temperature at a more even rate, minimizing thermal stresses." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.4

Pressurized thermal shock

Objectives 1.6, 1.7

"PTS is the shock experienced by a thick-walled vessel due to the combined stresses from a rapid temperature and/or pressure change." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.6

Nonuniform temperature distribution and the resulting differential expansion and contraction cause the stresses. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.6

"As the facilities get older in terms of full power operating years, the neutron radiation causes a change in the ductility of the vessel material, making it more susceptible to embrittlement." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.6
"Thus, if an older reactor vessel is cooled rapidly at high pressure, the potential for failure by cracking increases greatly." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.6
"Pressure in the reactor system raises the severity of the thermal shock due to the addition of stress from pressure." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.6
"Pressure in closed system raises the severity of thermal shock due to the additive effect of thermal and pressure tensile stresses on the inside reactor vessel wall." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.8
"In addition, the material toughness of the reactor vessel is reduced as the temperature rapidly decreases." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.6

Stress through the vessel wall

Objectives 1.7

"Stresses arising from coolant system pressure exerted against the inside vessel wall (where neutron fluence is greatest) are always tensile in nature." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.7

Stresses from temperature gradients can be tensile or compressive. The type depends on the location in the wall and reverses from heatup to cooldown. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.7

During heatup, the outer wall temperature lags the inner wall. At 1/4 T the temperature stresses are compressive, so the stresses tend to cancel. At 3/4 T the stresses from temperature and pressure are both tensile and reinforce each other. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.7

"For this reason the 3/4 T location is limiting during system heatup." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.7

During cooldown, the stresses at 3/4 T tend to cancel. At 1/4 T the pressure and temperature stresses are both tensile and reinforce each other. DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.7

"Thus, the 1/4 T location is limiting during system cooldown." DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.7

Transients with the greatest potential

Objectives 1.8

"Plant transients with greatest potential to cause PTS include:" DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.8

  • Plant scrams
  • Plant pressure excursions outside of normal pressure bands

Worked examples

Objective 1.3

Thermal stress in a constrained bar

A carbon steel bar is constrained at both ends. What is the thermal stress when it is heated from 60°F to 540°F?

  1. α = 5.8 x 10⁻⁶ per °F. E = 3.0 x 10⁷ lb/in.².
  2. ΔT = 540°F - 60°F = 480°F.
  3. F/A = EαΔT = (3.0 x 10⁷ lb/in.²)(5.8 x 10⁻⁶ per °F)(480°F).

Answer: Thermal stress = 8.4 x 10⁴ lb/in.², which is higher than the yield point.

DOE-HDBK-1017/2-93 MS-03 p.3
