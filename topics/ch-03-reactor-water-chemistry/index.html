DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93, Module 3.

Terminal objective

1.0 Without references, DESCRIBE the effects of radiation on reactor water and methods of treatment for the products. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.v

Enabling objectives

1.1 DESCRIBE the process of radiolytic decomposition and recombination of water. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.v

1.2 DESCRIBE the process of radiolytic decomposition and recombination of nitric acid and ammonia. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.v

1.3 STATE the advantage of maintaining excess hydrogen in reactor water. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.v

1.4 STATE the three sources of radioactivity in reactor water and each one’s decay product. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.v

  • a. Nine parameters controlled
  • b. Reason for controlling each parameter
  • c. Method of controlling each parameter

1.6 STATE the possible effects of abnormal chemistry on core conditions. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.v

Radiolysis of water

Objectives 1.1

Radiation interacts with coolant water mainly by ionization. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.1

"In the case of water, the overall effect of irradiation is shown in the following reaction." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.2

2H2O + radiation → 2H2 + O2 DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.2

"The net result of these reactions is simply the decomposition of water." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.4
"Primarily neutron and gamma radiation induce both the decomposition of water and the recombination of H2 and O2 to form water." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.4

In a closed system under pressure, H2 and O2 are confined, and an equilibrium state is reached. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.4

Excess hydrogen

Objectives 1.3

"To prevent the formation of oxygen in reactor coolant, hydrogen is added." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.5
"Thus, the addition of H2 to reactor coolant largely eliminates production of free oxygen." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.5
"the addition of excess hydrogen forces the equilibrium to the left, which requires that O2 be consumed." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.6

Normally, reactor coolant contains excess dissolved hydrogen, so there is no significant net decomposition of water by radiation. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.6

Knowledge check: Excess hydrogen

Objective 1.3Q495

What suppresses the radiation-driven decomposition of pure reactor water?

The answer is C. Radiation causes pure water to decompose into hydrogen and oxygen. The decomposition is suppressed by adding excess hydrogen. DOE-1015-V2 (DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93), Effects of Radiation on Water Chemistry, CH-03 p. 11

Air in the coolant: ammonia and nitric acid

Objectives 1.2

After oxygen from air is consumed, hydrogen and nitrogen can remain. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.7

"When exposed to radiation, however, the gases do react by the following reaction." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.7

3H2 + N2 ⇌ 2NH3 DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.7

"In most cases, less than about 10 percent of the nitrogen will be converted to ammonia." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.7

If a large amount of air depletes the hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen form nitric acid. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.7

2N2 + 5O2 + 2H2O → 4HNO3 DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.7

"Nitric acid (HNO3) produced by this reaction will neutralize any base contained in the coolant, and if sufficient acid is produced, the coolant will acquire an acidic pH." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.7

One solution would be to add more hydrogen, which reacts with the remaining oxygen. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.8

"Briefly, Reaction (3-13) is much more rapid than either of the others, and Reaction (3-16) is faster than Reaction (3-14)." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.9

Sources of radioactivity

Objectives 1.4

Fast neutron and proton reactions with oxygen isotopes produce radioactive nitrogen and fluorine isotopes. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.9

  • N-16 (7.13 second half-life) decays to O-16, with a high-energy gamma ray
  • N-17 (4.1 second half-life) decays to O-16, with a neutron and a gamma ray

DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.9

  • N-13 (10 minute half-life) decays to C-13
  • F-18 (112 minute half-life) decays to O-18
  • Tritium (12.3 year half-life) forms from deuterium

DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.10

N-16 and N-17 are carried outside the core and matter for shielding, but they decay quickly after shutdown. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.9

From a few minutes to about five hours after shutdown, N-13 and F-18 are the principal sources of radioactivity in the coolant. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.10

Controlled chemistry parameters

Objectives 1.5

"pH, dissolved oxygen, hydrogen, total gas content, conductivity, chlorides, fluorine, boron, and radioactivity." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.12
  • pH: inhibit corrosion and protect the corrosion film. Control by ion exchange and chemical addition
  • Dissolved oxygen: inhibit corrosion. Control by hydrogen or hydrazine addition
  • Hydrogen: scavenge oxygen and suppress radiolytic decomposition. Control by hydrogen addition and degassification
  • Total gas: protect pumps and indicate air in leakage. Control by degassification
  • Conductivity: minimize scale and indicate corrosion. Control by ion exchange, feed and bleed
  • Chlorides: preclude chloride stress corrosion. Control by ion exchange, feed and bleed
  • Fluorine: preclude corrosion of zirconium cladding. Control by ion exchange, feed and bleed
  • Boron: control reactivity. Control by boric acid addition
  • Radioactivity: indicate corrosion, crud bursts, fuel defects, and demineralizer performance

DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.13

"The reason for controlling pH in the reactor coolant system is to minimize and control corrosion." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.14
"Control of the dissolved oxygen content in the reactor facility system is of paramount importance because of its contribution to increased corrosion." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.15
"Hydrogen gas (H2) and hydrazine (N2H4) are the scavenging agents normally used to eliminate dissolved oxygen from the reactor coolant system." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.16

Hydrazine decomposes rapidly above about 200 °F, so hydrogen is used during hot operation. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.16

"Total gas is the sum of all gases contained in the coolant system" DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.18
"Conductivity of reactor facility water is measured to provide an indication of dissolved ionic substances in the coolant." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.19
"Low conductivity is also an indicator of a potential problem because, in high purity basic systems, the only possible cause of low conductivity is a low pH." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.21
"the type of greatest concern is chloride stress corrosion." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.21
"First, F- promotes corrosion of zirconium by a stress corrosion mechanism at the inner surface of the clad" DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.22
"Radioactivity of the reactor coolant system is monitored and controlled because it is a good indicator of several conditions that may occur in the system." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.23

Objective 1.5Q1140

Why is hydrazine not used as an oxygen scavenger during hot operation?

The answer is D. Because hydrazine decomposes rapidly above about 200 °F, hydrogen gas is used during hot operation. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.16

Objective 1.5Q1141

In a high purity basic system, what is the only possible cause of low conductivity?

The answer is C. In high purity basic systems, the only possible cause of low conductivity is a low pH. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.21

Report a problem
Objective 1.5Q1142

Why is chloride kept at the minimum practicable level?

The answer is B. The type of corrosion of greatest concern with chloride is chloride stress corrosion. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.21

Abnormal chemistry

Objectives 1.6

"When sufficient oxygen is added to deplete the hydrogen inventory, acidic pH results in those facilities where high (basic) pH is maintained." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.25

The reduced pH loosens the corrosion film, causing a crud burst, seen as increased radiation levels and conductivity and lower ion exchanger efficiency. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.25

"If a defect were present or a failure of a fuel element occurred, large amounts of fission products would potentially have a path to the coolant system." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.28

Gaseous radioactive levels would rise, and ion exchanger efficiency may fall because many fission products have lower affinity for the resin. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.28

The anion resin begins to decompose slowly at about 140 °F. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.28

Decomposition becomes rapid above 180 °F. The cation resin is stable up to about 250 °F. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.29

"Another significant property of TMA is its intense odor of dead fish." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.29

Overheating can raise chlorides and radioactivity, reduce ion exchanger effectiveness, and change pH. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.29

"If overheating occurs, the ion exchanger should be taken out of service immediately and the cause rectified." DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.29

Objective 1.6Q445

What can injection of air into the reactor coolant lead to?

The answer is A. Injection of air depletes the hydrogen inventory, which can lead to a pH swing towards the acidic end. DOE-1015-V2 (DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93), Chemistry Parameters, CH-03 p. 30

What is an indication of resin overheating, though not definitive?

The answer is D. TMA has an intense odor of dead fish, which may give an indication of resin overheating. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.29

What should be done if resin overheats?

The answer is C. If overheating occurs, the ion exchanger should be taken out of service immediately and the cause rectified. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.29

Summary

  • Radiation causes pure water to decompose into H2 and O2, and adding excess hydrogen suppresses it. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.11
  • Very large air additions may produce more nitric acid than the basic coolant can neutralize. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.11
  • Injection of air can deplete hydrogen and cause a pH swing, a crud burst, and higher radiation levels. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.30
  • Resin overheating decomposes the resin, and its products affect coolant chemistry. DOE-HDBK-1015/2-93 CH-03 p.30
