Matter and the atom

Objectives 1.1, 1.2, 1.3

"The term states of matter refers to the physical forms in which matter exists: solid, liquid, and gas." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.1

Solids have a definite shape and volume. Liquids have definite volumes but indefinite shapes. Gases have indefinite shape and volume. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.1

"An atom is composed of a positively-charged nucleus orbited by one or more negatively-charged particles called electrons." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.2
"Together the neutrons and protons give the nucleus its mass, but the proton alone gives the nucleus its positive charge." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.2
"They are very small, with a mass only 1/1835 the mass of a proton or neutron." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.3
  • Electron: relative mass 0.00055 amu, relative charge -1
  • Proton: relative mass 1.0 amu, relative charge +1
  • Neutron: relative mass 1.0 amu, relative charge 0

DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.3

The diameter of an atom is about 10⁻⁸ cm, and the nucleus is roughly 10,000 times smaller. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.3

"An atom is classified chemically by the number of protons in its nucleus." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.3
"Atoms of the same element with different atomic mass numbers are called isotopes." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.4

Atomic weight and the mole

Objectives 1.1

Atomic masses are given in atomic mass units (amu). 1 amu is defined as 1/12 the mass of the carbon-12 atom. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.4

The DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.4

"atomic weight of an element is defined as the weighted average of the masses of all of its natural occurring isotopes." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.4
"The weight of a molecule, the molecular weight, is the total mass of the individual atoms." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.7

Avogadro's number is 6.022 x 10²³. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.9

"In other words, a mole is Avogadro's number of anything." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.9
"The mass of a mole of atoms is called the gram atomic weight (GAW)." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.9
"The mass of a mole of molecules is called the molar mass or gram molecular weight(GMW)." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.10

The periodic table

Objectives 1.4, 1.5, 1.6

"Each horizontal row is called a period." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.14
"Elements with similar chemical properties appear in vertical columns called groups." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.14
"These are the metals, the non-metals, and the semi-metals." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.16
"Metals tend to lose electrons to form positive ions rather than to gain electrons and become negative ions." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.16
"Nonmetals tend to gain electrons to form negative ions rather than to lose electrons to form positive ions." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.17
"Their reactions with water range from violent with sodium and potassium to imperceptible with gold and platinum." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.17
"The light metals, which are soft, have a low density, are very reactive chemically, and are unsatisfactory as structural materials." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.17
"The transition metals, which are hard, have a high density, do not react readily, and are useful structural materials." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.17

Semi-metals exhibit metal properties under some conditions and non-metal properties under others. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.18

"The most accurate observation is that all elements within a particular group have similar physical and chemical properties." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.18
  • Group IA: Alkali Family, soft metals
  • Group IIA: Alkaline Earth Family
  • Group VIA: Oxygen Family
  • Group VIIA: Halogen Family
  • Group 0: inert gases, unreactive

DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.18

"The most active metals are the members of the Alkali Family" DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.19

Electron shells and valence

Objectives 1.7

Electrons occupy shells at discrete energy levels. Electrons always seek the lowest energy state available. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.19

Chemical reactions involve primarily the electrons in the outermost shell, called the valence shell. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.21

"The term valence (of an atom) is defined as the number of electrons an element gains or loses, or the number of pairs of electrons it shares when it interacts with other elements." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.21

For A group elements, the number of valence electrons is the same as the group number. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.21

"In general, the fewer electrons an element must lose, gain, or share to reach a stable shell structure, the more chemically active the element is." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.21

Chemical bonds

Objectives 2.1, 2.2

"An ionic bond is formed when one or more electrons is wholly transferred from one element to another, and the elements are held together by the force of attraction due to the opposing charges." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.24
"A covalent bond is formed when one or more electrons from an atom pair off with one or more electrons from another atom and form overlapping electron shells in which both atoms share the paired electrons." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.25

Covalent bonding can be single, double, or triple, depending on the number of pairs of electrons shared. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.25

"When the shared pair of electrons is not shared equally, one end of the bond is positive, and the other end is negative." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.26
"Water is an example of a polar molecule." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.27
"In the metallic bond, an atom achieves a more stable configuration by sharing the electrons in its outer shell with many other atoms." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.27

Valence electrons move among atoms in a metal crystal, which is why most metals are malleable and ductile. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.27

"These forces are generally believed to be caused by a temporary dipole, or unequal charge distribution, as electrons constantly move about in an atom, ion, or molecule." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.27

Van der Waals forces are small compared to the forces of chemical bonding. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.28

Chemical laws and combining elements

Objectives 2.3, 2.4, 2.5

The Law of Conservation of Mass: DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.32

"This law states that in a chemical reaction the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.32

The Law of Definite Proportions says that however a compound is prepared, DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.32

"it always contains the same elements in the same proportions by mass." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.32

The Law of Multiple Proportions says that if two elements form more than one compound, DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.32

"the masses of one of the elements combining with a fixed mass of the other are in a simple ratio to one another." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.32

Regardless of the type of bond, specific amounts of one element react with specific amounts of the element it combines with. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.33

The likelihood of elements forming compounds is strongly influenced by the valence shell and by the stability of the resulting molecule. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.35

Objectives 2.8, 2.9

"Density is the measure of the mass per unit volume of a material" DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.37
"Molarity is defined as moles of solute per liter of solution." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.37
"Normality (N) is defined as the number of equivalents of solute dissolved in one liter of solution." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.38
"One ppm equals one milligram of solute per liter of solution." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.39

One ppb is equal to one microgram solute per liter of solution. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.39

In a chemical equation, reactants are on the left of the arrow and products on the right. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.40

"a chemical equation must be balanced so that there are exactly the same number of atoms of each element on each side of the equation." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.40
  • Do not change a compound's correct formula
  • Start with the compound with the most atoms, usually not with hydrogen, oxygen, or a polyatomic ion
  • Balance each element with coefficients
  • Balance polyatomic ions, then hydrogen, then oxygen
  • Use whole number coefficients at the lowest ratios

DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.41

pH and pOH

Objectives 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4

pH = -log [H+] [H+] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.48

"The pOH of a solution is defined as the negative logarithm of the hydroxyl concentration, represented as [OH-] in moles/liter." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.49

pOH = -log [OH-] DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.49

For water solutions at 25 °C, the product of the hydrogen and hydroxyl concentrations is 1 x 10⁻¹⁴, so pH + pOH = 14. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.49

This product, Kw, is called the Ion Product Constant, Ionization Constant, or Dissociation Constant. It varies with temperature. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.50

"pH values less than 7 indicate an acidic solution and values greater than 7 indicate a basic or alkaline solution." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.51

The pH of pure water at 25 °C is 7. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-01 p.51

A silver bar has a mass of 1870 grams. One mole of silver is 107.87 grams. How many moles are in the bar?
