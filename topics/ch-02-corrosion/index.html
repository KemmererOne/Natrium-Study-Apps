These pages are tagged Water-system only: General corrosion of iron; Factors affecting corrosion rate; Chemical control of corrosion; Corrosion of aluminum; Crud and scale; Pitting and crevice corrosion; Chloride and caustic stress corrosion. Other pages apply to the sodium and salt systems.

Terminal objective

1.0 Without references, DESCRIBE the causes and effects of corrosion on metals and the type of chemistry used in a plant to minimize corrosion. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.v

Corrosion terms

Objectives 1.1

"Corrosion is the deterioration of a material due to interaction with its environment." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.2
"Rapid localized corrosion may lead to penetration of the metal containing the coolant." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.1

Corroded cladding can become brittle, and corrosion products can become radioactive in the core and deposit outside it. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.2

"Ionization is the process of adding electrons to or removing electrons from atoms or molecules, creating ions." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.2
"Conductivity is a measure of the ability of a substance to allow electron flow." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.2
"Electrolysis is the decomposition by electric current" DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.2
"General corrosion is the process whereby the surface of a metal undergoes a slow, relatively uniform; removal of material." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.6
"This chapter will concentrate on the corrosion of metals in water-based environments (electrolytes)." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.2

Electrochemical cells

Objectives 1.2

"Corrosion is electrochemical in nature because the corrosive chemical reactions involve transfer of charge." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.3

As metal ions go into solution, the metal becomes negatively charged with respect to the electrolyte, and a potential difference is produced. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.3

Oxidation sites become electrochemical cells of micro-electrodes, connected through the bulk of the metal. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.3

"In order for corrosion to occur, the micro-cells must also be connected through some conducting path external to the metal." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.4

Oxidation and reduction

Objectives 1.3, 1.4

"Oxidation is the process of losing electrons; reduction is the process of gaining electrons." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.5
"The metal atoms release electrons (are oxidized) and become positive ions. The site at which this occurs is known as the anode." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.5
"An oxidation process cannot take place without a simultaneous reduction (gain of electrons) process." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.5

The primary effect of the oxidation step is that atoms leave the metallic state, and the metal deteriorates. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.5

Oxidation occurs at anodic areas and reduction at cathodic areas. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.6

Passivity and polarization

Objectives 1.5, 1.6

"Passivity is the characteristic of a metal exhibited when that metal does not become active in the corrosion reaction." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.6
"Passivity is caused by the buildup of a stable, tenacious layer of metal oxide on the surface of the metal." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.6

Once formed, the film is a barrier. Reactants must diffuse through it, so corrosion decreases markedly or stops. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.6

"In electrochemical cells, the decrease in cell potential caused by the operation of the cell (current flow) is called polarization." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.7

Concentration polarization results from changes in concentrations. Activation polarization results from absorbed atomic hydrogen at the cathode. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.9

"Both concentration and activation polarization decrease the net oxidation-reduction reaction rate." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.9

General corrosion of iron

Objectives 1.7, 1.9

"The two conditions typically required for a metal to undergo general corrosion are: 1) metal and water in the same environment, and 2) a chemical reaction between the metal and water that forms an oxide." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.11

Iron ions combine with hydroxyl ions at the surface, forming FeO, then layers of Fe3O4 and Fe2O3 build up. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.12

"The three products formed from general corrosion of iron are FeO, Fe2O3, and Fe3O4." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.20
"the oxide layer represents a barrier to continued corrosion and tends to slow the corrosion rate." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.13

Factors affecting corrosion rate

Objectives 1.8

  • Temperature: corrosion rates increase as temperature increases
  • Water velocity: extremely high velocity removes the protective oxide
  • Oxygen: increases the corrosion rate of iron in water
  • Condition of the surface: deposits and irregular surfaces speed local corrosion

DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.13

"Water velocities of 30 to 40 ft per second are usually considered to cause erosion." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.13
"The presence of oxygen in water to which iron is exposed increases the corrosion rate." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.13

Oxygen reacts rapidly with the polarizing layer of atomic hydrogen on the oxide layer. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.13

"that remove the absorbed atomic hydrogen are called depolarizers." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.14

In aerated water at room temperature, the corrosion rate is relatively independent of pH from 4 to 10. Below pH 4, ferrous oxide is soluble. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.15

"Deposits, scale, or irregular surfaces create areas on the metal where local corrosion can initiate and proceed at a faster rate than normal." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.16
"Dissolved solids tend to make it easier for current to flow, which results in a higher corrosion rate." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.20

Chemical control of corrosion

Objectives 1.10, 1.11

A tightly adhering corrosion film inhibits further corrosion, so great care is taken during initial fill. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.16

"This process, referred to as pretreatment, or pickling, involves careful control of reactor coolant water chemistry and temperature during the pretreatment period." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.16
  • Passivators and inhibitors
  • Cathodic protection
  • Removing corrosive agents
  • Chemical addition

DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.21

Passivators, if added to water, undergo reduction at the metal surface. A common inhibitor is potassium chromate. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.16

"The use of cathodic protection, supplying an external electric current to the iron so that it acts as a cathode and has no anodic areas, is another method of preventative chemical control." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.16

Removal uses deaerators for dissolved oxygen, and softening and demineralization for dissolved solids. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.17

Chemical addition includes filming amines, phosphates, and sodium hydroxide. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.17

Crud and scale

Objectives 1.13, 1.14, 1.15

"corrosion products in the form of finely divided, insoluble oxide particles called crud become suspended in the reactor coolant or loosely adhere to metal surfaces." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.22
"The most undesirable characteristic of crud, however, is that it becomes activated when exposed to radiation." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.22
  • Fouling of coolant flow paths
  • Fouling of heat transfer surfaces
  • High general background (ambient) radiation levels
  • Radiation hot spots
  • Radioactive waste disposal

DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.25

"The release of crud in this fashion is termed a crud burst." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.23
  • Increased oxygen concentration
  • Reduced (or significantly changed) pH
  • Large temperature change
  • Physical shock

DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.26

Physical shocks include starting, stopping, or changing pump speeds, a reactor scram, or a relief valve lift. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.23

"Another corrosion byproduct is scale, which is made up of deposits on surfaces from the formation of insoluble compounds from normally soluble salts." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.23

Galvanic corrosion

Objectives 1.13, 1.16, 1.17, 1.18, 1.19

"Galvanic corrosion is the corrosion that results when two dissimilar metals with different potentials are placed in electrical contact in an electrolyte." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.23

A difference in electrical potential drives current through the electrolyte. The larger the difference, the greater the probability of galvanic corrosion. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.23

"The less resistant, active metal becomes the anodic corrosion site. The stronger, more noble metal is cathodic and protected." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.23
"Two locations susceptible to galvanic corrosion is a piping transition from one metal to another and a sacrificial anode (such as zinc)." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.23
"The metal that corrodes to protect another metal is called a sacrificial anode." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.24
  • Cathodic protection with a third metal, or using metals close on the activity series
  • Choosing relative surface areas
  • Separating dissimilar metals with a non-conducting material
  • Separating the metals from a conductive environment
  • Use of poorly conducting electrolytes

DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.26

Pitting and crevice corrosion

Objectives 1.20, 1.21, 1.22, 1.23

"Pitting corrosion occurs where the anodic site becomes fixed in a small area and the formation of holes (deep attack) in an otherwise unaffected area takes place." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.28
"Crevice corrosion is a type of pitting corrosion that occurs specifically within the low flow region of a crevice." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.28

Loss of metal occurs at the electrode that is deficient in oxygen. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.28

"Pitting corrosion requires two conditions to occur, low flow and areas of both high and low oxygen concentration." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.36
"Pitting and crevice corrosion are a major hazard to a nuclear facility because of the rapid penetration of the metal with little overall loss of mass." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.31
  • Avoiding stagnant or low flow conditions
  • Using metals and alloys that are less susceptible
  • Avoiding agents that cause pitting, such as chlorides and oxygen
  • Designing the system so no crevices are present

DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.31

Stress corrosion cracking

Objectives 1.20, 1.24, 1.25, 1.26, 1.27

"Stress corrosion cracking (SCC) is a type of intergranular attack corrosion that occurs at the grain boundaries under tensile stress." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.31
"Stress corrosion cracking occurs when three conditions are met; the alloy is susceptible to stress corrosion cracking, the alloy is exposed to specific environment, and the alloy is in a stressed condition." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.36
"According to the most widely accepted theory, stress corrosion cracking is caused by a process called chemisorption." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.31
"The formation of this chemisorbed layer greatly reduces the attraction between neighboring metal atoms." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.31

Most pure metals are immune to this type of attack. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.31

"SCC is of great concern because it can readily crack metal of appreciable thickness." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.31

The crack can lead to a serious failure, with contamination, loss of coolant, and loss of pressure. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.31

It is prevented by proper design, reducing stress, and avoiding stagnant areas and crevices where chlorides and hydroxides might concentrate. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.37

Chloride and caustic stress corrosion

Objectives 1.28

"The three conditions that must be present for chloride stress corrosion to occur are as follows." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.32
  • Chloride ions are present in the environment
  • Dissolved oxygen is present in the environment
  • Metal is under tensile stress

DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.32

It is controlled by maintaining low chloride ion and oxygen content and using low carbon steels. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.32

Caustic stress corrosion attacks mild steels and stainless steels exposed to concentrated caustic environments under tensile stress. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.34

"In stress cracking that is induced by a caustic environment, the presence of dissolved oxygen is not necessary for the cracking to occur." DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.34
"Available data indicates that caustic concentrations greater than 10,000 ppm, and probably up to 50,000 ppm, are required to induce caustic stress cracking" DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.35

Summary

  • Corrosion is electrochemical in nature because the reactions involve a transfer of charge. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.9
  • Passivity and polarization both slow or stop the corrosion process. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.10
  • The three products formed from general corrosion of iron are FeO, Fe2O3, and Fe3O4. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.20
  • Crud problems include fouling, radiation levels, hot spots, and waste disposal. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.25
  • In galvanic corrosion, the active metal becomes anodic and the noble metal becomes cathodic. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.26
  • Stress corrosion cracking can readily crack metal of appreciable thickness. DOE-HDBK-1015/1-93 CH-02 p.37
