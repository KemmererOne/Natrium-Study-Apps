Heat, temperature, and work

Objectives 1.1, 1.2, 1.3

"Temperature is a measure of the amount of energy possessed by the molecules of a substance." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.1

Temperature is a relative measure of how hot or cold a substance is and can be used to predict the direction of heat transfer. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.1

"Heat is energy in transit." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.1

Heat transfer occurs at the molecular level as a result of a temperature difference. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.1

Work is the transfer of energy resulting from a force acting through a distance. Heat is energy transferred as the result of a temperature difference. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.2

"Heat can be transferred into or out of a system and work can be done on or by a system, but a system cannot contain or store either heat or work." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.2

When a temperature difference exists across a boundary, the Second Law of Thermodynamics indicates: DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.2

"the natural flow of energy is from the hotter body to the colder body." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.2

The Second Law implies that heat will not transfer from a colder to a hotter body without some external source of energy. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.5

Objectives 1.4

"Conduction involves the transfer of heat by the interactions of atoms or molecules of a material through which the heat is being transferred." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.2
"Convection involves the transfer of heat by the mixing and motion of macroscopic portions of a fluid." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.2
"Radiation, or radiant heat transfer, involves the transfer of heat by electromagnetic radiation that arises due to the temperature of a body." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.2

Conduction problems usually study a solid, convection problems involve a fluid medium, and radiation problems involve surfaces separated by a gas, vapor, or vacuum. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.6

Heat transfer terms

Objectives 1.5

"The heat flux can be determined by dividing the heat transfer rate by the area through which the heat is being transferred." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.3

Heat flux = Q̇ / A, in Btu/hr-ft² DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.3

Thermal conductivity (k) is measured in Btu/hr-ft-°F. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.3

"It is a measure of a substance’s ability to transfer heat through a solid by conduction." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.3
"A precise temperature change between two fluids across the heat exchanger is best represented by the log mean temperature difference" DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.3

∆Tlm = (∆T2 - ∆T1) / ln(∆T2 / ∆T1) DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.3

∆T2 is the larger temperature difference between the two fluid streams at either end, and ∆T1 is the smaller. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.3

"The convective heat transfer coefficient is sometimes referred to as a film coefficient" DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.4

It represents the thermal resistance of a relatively stagnant layer of fluid between a heat transfer surface and the fluid medium. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.4

"The overall heat transfer coefficient combines the heat transfer coefficient of the two heat exchanger fluids and the thermal conductivity of the heat exchanger tubes." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.4

Q̇ = UoAo∆To DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.4

"For flow adjacent to a hot or cold surface, Tb is the temperature of the fluid that is "far" from the surface" DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.4

Conduction through a flat wall

Objectives 1.6, 1.7

Conduction depends on the driving force of temperature difference and the resistance to heat transfer. Fourier's Law of Conduction is the most common way to correlate them. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.6

Q̇ = kA(∆T / ∆x) DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.6

"It is possible to compare heat transfer to current flow in electrical circuits." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.9

Heat transfer rate is like current flow, and temperature difference is the potential that drives it. The Fourier equation can be written in a form similar to Ohm's Law. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.9

Heat flux = ∆T / Rth, with Rth = ∆x / k DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.9

Heat exchanger flow paths

Objectives 1.11

"The most common design has one fluid flowing through metal tubes and the other fluid flowing around the tubes." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.30
"On either side of the tube, heat is transferred by convection. Heat is transferred through the tube wall by conduction." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.30
"A counter-flow heat exchanger is one in which the direction of the flow of one of the working fluids is opposite to the direction to the flow of the other fluid." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.31
"In a parallel flow exchanger, both fluids in the heat exchanger flow in the same direction." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.31
"Under comparable conditions, more heat is transferred in a counter-flow arrangement than in a parallel flow heat exchanger." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.31

Parallel flow has a large temperature difference at the ends, which causes large thermal stresses. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.32

"the temperature of the cold fluid exiting the heat exchanger never exceeds the lowest temperature of the hot fluid." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.32
"the outlet temperature of the cold fluid can approach the highest temperature of the hot fluid (the inlet temperature)." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.33

A counter-flow exchanger's more uniform temperature difference minimizes thermal stresses and gives a more uniform rate of heat transfer. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.33

A parallel flow design is advantageous when two fluids must be brought to nearly the same temperature. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.33

Boiling heat transfer

Objectives 1.15

"In nucleate boiling, steam bubbles form at the heat transfer surface and then break away and are carried into the main stream of the fluid." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.40

Once in the main fluid stream, the bubbles collapse, because the bulk temperature is below the surface temperature. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.40

As temperature rises or pressure drops, the bulk fluid can reach saturation and bubbles no longer collapse. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.41

"This phenomenon is referred to as bulk boiling." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.41

When more bubbles form than can be carried away, they group together, DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.41

"covering small areas of the heat transfer surface with a film of steam. This is known as partial film boiling." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.41

Steam has a lower convective heat transfer coefficient than water, so the patches insulate the surface. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.41

"The condition after the stable steam blanket has formed is referred to as film boiling." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.41
"The point of transition from nucleate boiling to film boiling is called the point of departure from nucleate boiling, commonly written as DNB." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.42
"The heat flux associated with DNB is commonly called the critical heat flux (CHF)." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.42

Decay heat

Objectives 2.7, 2.8

All radioactive materials in the reactor at shutdown continue to decay and release energy. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.52

"This release of energy by the decay of fission products is called decay heat." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.52

The amount present depends on the power levels at which the reactor operated and the time spent at those levels. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.52

"Typically, the amount of decay heat that will be present in the reactor immediately following shutdown will be roughly 7% of the power level that the reactor operated at prior to shutdown." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.52
"Decay heat may decrease to about 2% of the pre-shutdown power level within the first hour after shutdown and to 1% within the first day." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.52

It will continue to decrease more slowly, and is significant weeks and even months after shutdown. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.52

"If adequate heat removal is not available, decay heat will increase the temperatures in the core to the point that fuel melting and core damage will occur." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.53

Q̇ = Q̇o (1/2)^(time / half-life) DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.53

The single half-life is a rough approximation of the overall decay of the core. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.53

"If the mass of the coolant and the specific heat of the coolant are known, the heat generation rate can be accurately calculated." DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.54

Q̇ = m cp (∆T / ∆t) DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.54

1000 Btu/hr passes through a 1 ft² slab 1 in. thick with k = 0.12 Btu/hr-ft-°F. Find ∆T.

  1. ∆T = Q̇(∆x)/(kA)
  2. ∆T = 1000 (1/12 ft)/(0.12)(1 ft²)

Answer: 694 °F

Objective 1.7

Thermal resistance of a composite wall

A wall has 1 in. of copper (k = 240), 1/8 in. of asbestos (k = 0.048), and 2 in. of fiberglass (k = 0.022). Find each layer's thermal resistance.

  1. R = ∆x/k with ∆x in feet
  2. RCu = (1/12)/240
  3. Rasb = (0.125/12)/0.048
  4. Rfib = (2/12)/0.022

Answer: 0.000347, 0.2170, and 7.5758 hr-ft²-°F/Btu

DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.10

Objective 1.8

Heat transfer through a pipe wall

A 35 ft stainless steel pipe has an inner diameter of 0.92 ft and an outer diameter of 1.08 ft. Inner surface is 122 °F, outer is 118 °F, and k = 108 Btu/hr-ft-°F. Find the heat transfer rate.

  1. Q̇ = 2πkL(Th - Tc)/ln(ro/ri)
  2. Q̇ = 6.28 (108)(35)(122 - 118)/ln(0.54/0.46)

Answer: 5.92 x 10⁵ Btu/hr

DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.13

Objective 1.8

Interior temperature of a pipe

A 10 ft pipe has inner radius 1 in. and outer radius 1.25 in., with an outer surface at 250 °F. Heat transfer is 30,000 Btu/hr and k = 25 Btu/hr-ft-°F. Find the interior temperature.

  1. Th = Tc + Q̇ ln(ro/ri)/(2πkL)
  2. Th = 250 + 30,000 ln(1.25)/(2(3.14)(25)(10))

Answer: 254 °F

DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.14

Objective 1.9

Bulk temperature from convection

Insulation 1 in. thick (k = 0.8) has 600 °F on the wall side and 105 °F on the outer surface. The convection coefficient is 950 Btu/hr-ft²-°F. Find the bulk temperature.

  1. Heat flux = 0.8 (600 - 105)/(1/12) = 4752 Btu/hr-ft²
  2. Tb = 105 - 4752/950

Answer: 100 °F

DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.24

Objective 1.13

Log mean temperature difference

A counterflow exchanger heats a cold fluid from 120 °F to 310 °F. The hot fluid enters at 500 °F and leaves at 400 °F. Find the LMTD.

  1. ∆T2 = 400 - 120 = 280 °F
  2. ∆T1 = 500 - 310 = 190 °F
  3. ∆Tlm = (280 - 190)/ln(280/190)

Answer: 232 °F

DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.36

Objective 1.14

Overall coefficient of a thin tube

Find Uo with h1 = 2000, h2 = 1500, a 0.1 in. wall, and k = 200.

  1. Uo = 1/(1/h1 + ∆r/k + 1/h2)
  2. Uo = 1/(1/2000 + (0.1/12)/200 + 1/1500)

Answer: 827.6 Btu/hr-ft²-°F

Objective 2.4

Average linear power density

A 3400 MW core has 12 ft fuel rods, 264 rods per assembly, and 193 assemblies. Find the average linear power density.

  1. Average = total thermal power / total fuel rod length
  2. 3.4 x 10⁶ kW / 12 (264)(193)

Answer: 5.56 kW/ft

Objective 2.3

Maximum linear power density

With a nuclear heat flux hot channel factor of 1.83 and an average of 5.56 kW/ft, find the maximum.

  1. Maximum = HFHCF x average
  2. 1.83 (5.56) kW/ft

Answer: 10.18 kW/ft

Objective 2.8

Decay heat after 12 hours

Effective power at shutdown was 120 MW. Decay heat at shutdown is 7% of it, with a 1 hr half life. How much heat removal is needed 12 hours later?

  1. (120 MW)(0.07) = 8.4 MW at shutdown
  2. 8.4 MW (1/2)^(12 hr / 1 hr) = 2.05 x 10⁻³ MW
  3. Convert with 3.413 x 10⁶ Btu/hr per MW

Answer: 7000 Btu/hr

Objective 2.8

Decay heat from a heatup rate

A primary system with 24,000 lbm of coolant (cp = 0.8 Btu/lbm-°F) heats up at 0.8 °F/hr with cooling secured. Each heat exchanger removes 12,000 Btu/hr. Is one enough?

  1. Q̇ = (24,000)(0.8)(0.8 °F / 1 hr) = 15,360 Btu/hr
  2. Compare with 12,000 Btu/hr

Answer: No. One heat exchanger will not be sufficient.

Summary

  • Heat is energy transferred as a result of a temperature difference. Temperature is a measure of the molecular energy in a substance. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.5
  • Heat flux is the rate of heat transfer per unit area. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.5
  • Fourier's Law of Conduction solves rectangular and cylindrical problems, and conduction problems can use electrical analogies. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.17
  • When conduction and convection both occur, the overall heat transfer coefficient must be used. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.25
  • Counter-flow heat exchangers typically remove more heat than parallel flow heat exchangers. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.39
  • Regenerative heat exchangers return energy to the system. Non-regenerative heat exchangers reject heat to the surroundings. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.39
  • The thermal power of a reactor is directly related to coolant mass flow rate and the temperature difference across the core. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.51
  • Decay heat depends on the reactor's power history, and methods for removing it are closed loop or once through. DOE-HDBK-1012/2-92 HT-02 p.57
