Stress

Objectives 1.1, 1.2

"Stress is the internal resistance of a material to the distorting effects of an external force or load." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.6

Stress: σ = F/A, where F is the applied force and A is the cross-sectional area perpendicular to the force. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.2

"There are many types of stresses, but they can all be generally classified in one of six categories: residual stresses, structural stresses, pressure stresses, flow stresses, thermal stresses, and fatigue stresses." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.2
"Tensile stress is that type of stress in which the two sections of material on either side of a stress plane tend to pull apart or elongate as illustrated in Figure 1(a)." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.4
"Compressive stress is the reverse of tensile stress." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.4

In compressive stress, adjacent parts of the material tend to press against each other through a stress plane. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.4

"Shear stress exists when two parts of a material tend to slide across each other in any typical plane of shear upon application of force parallel to that plane as illustrated in Figure 1(c)." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.4

The plane of a tensile or compressive stress lies perpendicular to the axis of the force. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.4

"The plane of a shear stress lies in the plane of the force system from which it originates." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.4
"The ability of a material to react to compressive stress or pressure is called compressibility." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.5

Strain

Objectives 1.3, 1.4, 1.5, 1.6

"Strain is the proportional dimensional change, or the intensity or degree of distortion, in a material under stress." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.10

Strain: ε = δ/L, where δ is the total elongation and L is the original length. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.7

"Strain may take two forms; elastic strain and plastic deformation." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.8
"Elastic strain is a transitory dimensional change that exists only while the initiating stress is applied and disappears immediately upon removal of the stress." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.8
"Plastic deformation (or plastic strain) is a dimensional change that does not disappear when the initiating stress is removed." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.8

With elastic strain, the atoms all return to their original positions when the stress is removed, and no permanent deformation occurs. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.8

"The state of stress just before plastic strain begins to appear is known as the proportional limit, or elastic limit, and is defined by the stress level and the corresponding value of elastic strain." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.8

Beyond the proportional limit, the deformation consists of both elastic and plastic strains. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.8

When metal experiences strain, its volume remains constant, so if one dimension increases, another must decrease. There are a few exceptions, such as strain hardening. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.9

Lattices with closely packed planes allow more plastic deformation, and cubic lattices allow slippage more easily than non-cubic lattices. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.9

"A face-centered cubic crystal structure will deform more readily under load before breaking than a body-centered cubic structure." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.9

The BCC lattice, as in α-iron, is cubic but not closely packed and forms strong metals. The FCC lattice, as in γ-iron, is cubic and closely packed and forms more ductile materials. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.9

Hooke's Law and Young's Modulus

Objectives 1.7, 1.8, 1.9

"Hooke's Law states that in the elastic range of a material strain is proportional to stress." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.14
"The elongation of the bar is directly proportional to the tensile force and the length of the bar and inversely proportional to the cross-sectional area and the modulus of elasticity." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.11

δ = PL/AE, where P is the force, L the length of the bar, A the cross-sectional area, and E the Modulus of Elasticity. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.11

"Young's Modulus (Elastic Modulus) is the ratio of stress to strain, or the gradient of the stress-strain graph." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.14

E = σ/ε DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.12

Young's Modulus is measured in the same units as stress, pounds per square inch, because strain is a ratio of two lengths. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.13

  • Aluminum: 1.0 x 10⁷ psi
  • Stainless steel: 2.9 x 10⁷ psi
  • Carbon steel: 3.0 x 10⁷ psi

DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.13

Stress-strain curves

Objectives 1.10, 1.11, 1.12, 1.13

"The Bulk Modulus of Elasticity is the elastic response to hydrostatic pressure and equilateral tension or the volumetric response to hydrostatic pressure and equilateral tension." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.16

A tensile test applies a gradually increasing tension force to a sample until it is pulled apart, measuring load and elongation. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.16

"Fracture point is the point where the material fractures due to plastic deformation." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.16
  • Point 2: proportional limit (PL) or elastic limit
  • Point 3: yield strength (YS) or yield point
  • Point 4: ultimate strength
  • Point 5: fracture point, at which failure occurs

DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.18

"Hooke's Law applies between Points 1 and 2." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.19
"Elastic region is between Points 1 and 2." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.19
"Plastic region is between Points 2 and 5." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.19

If stress is removed in the elastic region, the material returns to its original length. In the plastic region, it does not. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.18

Figure 3 is typical of a ductile material, where the plastic region is great and the material will bear more strain before fracture. Figure 4 is typical of a brittle material, where the plastic region is small. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.18

"Ductile material will deform (elongate) more than brittle material, shown in the figures within the text." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.19

Strength

Objectives 1.14, 1.15

"Strength is the ability of a material to resist deformation." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.20

The strength of a material is the maximum nominal stress it can sustain, and is always qualified by the type of stress, such as tensile, compressive, or shear strength. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.20

"Ultimate tensile strength (UTS) is the maximum resistance to fracture." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.28

UTS equals the maximum load divided by the area of the original cross section. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.21

"Yield strength is the stress at which a predetermined amount of permanent deformation occurs." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.28

When a force is applied, layers of atoms move in relation to adjacent layers, which is slip. Grain boundaries tend to prevent slip, and smaller grains give a larger grain boundary area. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.21

"Decreasing the grain size through cold or hot working of the metal tends to retard slip and thus increases the strength of the metal." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.21
"An increase in slip will decrease the strength of a material." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.28

Ductility

Objectives 1.14, 1.16, 1.17

"Ductility is the ability of a material to deform easily upon the application of a tensile force, or the ability of a material to withstand plastic deformation without rupture." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.28

Percent elongation and reduction of area are both indicators of ductility. Reduction of area is the better indicator, because it is measured at the minimum diameter of the neck. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.25

"An increase in temperature will increase ductility." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.25
"Ductility decreases with lower temperatures, cold working, and irradiation." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.28

A decrease in temperature causes a change from ductile to brittle behavior. Heating a cold-worked metal to or above the temperature at which its atoms return to their equilibrium positions increases ductility, and this is called annealing. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.25

"Ductility is desirable in high temperature and high pressure applications." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.28

High ductility in these applications helps prevent brittle fracture. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.25

Malleability, toughness, and hardness

Objectives 1.14

"Malleability is the ability of a metal to exhibit large deformation or plastic response when being subjected to compressive force." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.28
"Toughness describes how a material reacts under sudden impacts." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.28
"It is defined as the work required to deform one cubic inch of metal until it fractures." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.28

Toughness is measured by the Charpy test or the Izod test, which use a notched sample and a swinging pendulum. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.26

The more energy the specimen absorbs, the smaller the upward swing of the pendulum and the tougher the material. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.26

"Hardness is the property of a material that enables it to resist plastic deformation, penetration, indentation, and scratching." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.28

Resistance to wear by friction or erosion generally increases with hardness. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.27

In concentrations of less than 5%, nickel raises the toughness and ductility of steel without raising the hardness. Chromium forms a carbide that hardens the metal. Stainless steels are alloy steels containing at least 12% chromium. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.27

Heat treatment and working

Objectives 1.18

During manufacture, varying the rate of cooling (quenching) controls grain size and grain patterns, to produce different levels of hardness and tensile strength. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.29

"Generally, the faster a metal is cooled, the smaller the grain sizes will be." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.29

This makes the metal harder. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.29

"Hardness and tensile strength increase in heat-treated steel; toughness and ductility decrease." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.31

Annealing heats a component slowly to an elevated temperature, holds it there for a long time, then cools it. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.30

  • Softens the steel and improves ductility
  • Relieves internal stresses from previous processes
  • Refines the grain structure

DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.30

Cold work is plastic deformation carried out so that strain hardening is not relieved, and it decreases ductility. Hot working deforms metals above their recrystallization temperature, and strain hardening does not occur. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.30

Galvanic corrosion

Objectives 1.20

"Corrosion is the deterioration of a material due to interaction with its environment." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.32

All metals and alloys are subject to corrosion. It cannot be eliminated, but it can be controlled. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.32

"Galvanic corrosion occurs when two dissimilar metals with different potentials are placed in electrical contact in an electrolyte." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.33

A difference in electrical potential drives current through the electrolyte, and this current corrodes one of the metals. The larger the potential difference, the greater the probability of galvanic corrosion. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.33

"The less resistant, more active one becomes the anodic (negative) corrosion site." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.33

The stronger, more noble metal is cathodic and protected. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.33

"Galvanic corrosion is of particular concern in design and material selection." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.33
"Material selection is important because different metals come into contact with each other and may form galvanic cells." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.33
"Design is important to minimize differing flow conditions and resultant areas of corrosion buildup." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.33

Loose corrosion products are important because they can be transported to the reactor core and irradiated. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.33

Hydrogen embrittlement of steel

Objectives 1.21

"Hydrogen embrittlement is the process by which steel loses its ductility and strength due to tiny cracks that result from the internal pressure of hydrogen (H2) or methane gas (CH4), which forms at the grain boundaries." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.37
"The conditions required for hydrogen embrittlement in steel is the presence of hydrogen dissolved in the water and the carbon in the steel." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.39

Hydrogen diffuses along the grain boundaries and combines with the carbon in the steel to form methane gas, which is not mobile. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.37

"The methane gas collects in small voids along the grain boundaries where it builds up enormous pressures that initiate cracks and decrease the ductility of the steel." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.39

If the metal is under a high tensile stress, brittle failure can occur. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.38

"Hydrogen embrittlement is not a permanent condition." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.38

If cracking does not occur and no more hydrogen is generated at the surface, the hydrogen can rediffuse from the steel, so that ductility is restored. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.38

Hydrogen embrittlement of zirconium alloys

Objectives 1.22

Zirconium reacts with water, and part of the hydrogen produced combines with the zirconium to form zirconium hydride platelets. The metal then becomes embrittled, and cracks begin in the platelets. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.38

"Studies at Westinghouse, Batelle, and elsewhere have revealed that the nickel in the zircaloy-2 was responsible for the hydrogen pickup." DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.38
"Zircaloy-4 is less susceptible to hydrogen embrittlement than zircaloy-2 because:" DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.39
  • Zircaloy-4 contains less nickel
  • The introduction of niobium into zircaloy-4 reduces the amount of hydrogen absorption in the metal

Objective 1.9

Calculate elongation

What is the elongation of 200 in. of aluminum wire with a 0.01 square in. area if it supports a weight of 100 lb?

  1. Use Hooke's Law: δ = PL/AE.
  2. E for aluminum is 1.0 x 10⁷ lb/in.².
  3. δ = (100 lb)(200 in.) / (0.01 in.²)(1.0 x 10⁷ lb/in.²).

Answer: δ = 0.2 in.

Summary

  • Stress is the internal resistance of a material to the distorting effects of an external force or load, and its three types are tensile, compressive, and shear. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.6
  • Strain takes two forms: elastic strain, which disappears when the stress is removed, and plastic deformation, which does not. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.10
  • Hooke's Law states that in the elastic range of a material strain is proportional to stress. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.14
  • Hooke's Law applies between Points 1 and 2 of the stress-strain curve, and the plastic region is between Points 2 and 5. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.19
  • Ductility decreases with lower temperatures, cold working, and irradiation. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.28
  • Hardness and tensile strength increase in heat-treated steel, while toughness and ductility decrease. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.31
  • Welding produces compressive and tensile stresses, which are minimized by heat sink welding and annealing. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.31
  • Galvanic corrosion occurs when dissimilar metals exist at different electrical potentials in the presence of an electrolyte. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.36
  • Zircaloy-4 is less susceptible to hydrogen embrittlement than zircaloy-2 because it contains less nickel and has niobium added. DOE-HDBK-1017/1-93 MS-02 p.39
