Mass, volume, and density

Objectives 1.1, 1.2

The mass of a body is the measure of the amount of material present in it. Its weight is the force exerted when that mass is accelerated in a gravitational field. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.1

wt = mg / gc, with g = 32.17 ft/sec² and gc = 32.17 lbm-ft/lbf-sec² DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.1

The mass of a body stays constant even if the gravitational acceleration acting on it changes. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.1

"The specific volume (ν) of a substance is the total volume (V) of that substance divided by the total mass (m) of that substance" DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.3
"The density ( ρ ) of a substance is the reciprocal of its specific volume (ν)." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.3
"Specific gravity (S.G.) is a measure of the relative density of a substance as compared to the density of water at a standard temperature." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.4

Because the density of a fluid varies with temperature, specific gravities must be specified at particular temperatures. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.4

"Humidity is the amount of moisture (water vapor) in the air." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.4

Absolute humidity is the mass of water vapor per unit volume of air. Relative humidity is the water vapor present divided by the maximum the air could contain at that temperature, as a percentage. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.4

"An intensive property is independent of the amount of mass. The value of an extensive property varies directly with the mass." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.4

Temperature, pressure, specific volume, and density are intensive. Mass and total volume are extensive. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.4

Temperature scales

Objectives 1.3, 1.4, 1.5

"Temperature is a measure of the molecular activity of a substance." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.6

The greater the movement of molecules, the higher the temperature. Temperature can be used to predict the direction of heat transfer. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.6

The Celsius scale has 100 units between the freezing point and boiling point of water at standard atmospheric pressure, and the Fahrenheit scale has 180. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.6

°F = 32.0 + (9/5)°C °C = (°F - 32.0)(5/9) DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.7

The Kelvin scale is the absolute scale that corresponds to Celsius, and the Rankine scale is the absolute scale that corresponds to Fahrenheit. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.7

"The zero points on both absolute scales represent the same physical state." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.7

°R = °F + 460 °K = °C + 273 DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.7

  • Absolute zero: -460 °F or -273 °C
  • Freezing point of water: 32 °F or 0 °C
  • Boiling point of water: 212 °F or 100 °C

DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.12

Pressure scales

Objectives 1.3, 1.6, 1.7

"Pressure is a measure of the force exerted per unit area on the boundaries of a substance (or system)." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.9

Pressure is caused by the collisions of molecules with the boundaries of the system. It is frequently measured in lbf/in² (psi). DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.9

"When pressure is measured relative to a perfect vacuum, it is called absolute pressure (psia); when measured relative to atmospheric pressure (14.7 psi), it is called gauge pressure (psig)." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.9

Almost all pressure gauges register zero when open to the atmosphere, so they measure the difference between the fluid pressure and the surrounding air. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.9

"If the pressure is below that of the atmosphere, it is designated as a vacuum." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.9

Pabs = Patm + Pgauge Pabs = Patm - Pvac DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.10

  • 14.7 psia = 408 inches of water
  • 14.7 psia = 29.9 inches of mercury
  • 1 inch of mercury = 25.4 millimeters of mercury
  • 1 millimeter of mercury = 10³ microns of mercury

DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.11

A system has an absolute pressure of 20 psia. What is its gauge pressure?

A gauge reads 20 inches of mercury vacuum. With atmospheric pressure at 15 psia, what is the absolute pressure?

Work and heat

Objectives 1.8

"Work is a form of energy, but it is energy in transit." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.18

Work is not a property of a system. A system contains no work. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.18

W = Fd DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.19

"A positive value for work indicates that work is done by the system on its surroundings; a negative value indicates that work is done on the system by its surroundings." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.19
"Heat, like work, is energy in transit." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.19

Heat transfer occurs at the molecular level as a result of a temperature difference. In engineering, the unit of heat is the British thermal unit (Btu). DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.19

"A positive value for heat indicates that heat is added to the system by its surroundings." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.20
"The heat added to or removed from a substance to produce a change in its temperature is called sensible heat." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.20
"Latent heat is the amount of heat added to or removed from a substance to produce a change in phase." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.21
"When latent heat is added, no temperature change occurs." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.21

The latent heat of fusion changes phase between solid and liquid. The latent heat of vaporization changes phase between liquid and vapor, and is sometimes called the latent heat of condensation. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.21

"The heat capacity of a substance per unit mass is called the specific heat (cp) of the substance." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.21

cp = Q / (m∆T), so Q = m cp ∆T DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.21

Heat and work are both boundary phenomena. Both represent energy crossing the system boundary, and systems never possess either one. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.22

Thermodynamic processes

Objectives 1.12

The path of the succession of states through which a system passes is called the thermodynamic process. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.28

When a system returns to its initial values after a number of changes in state, it has undergone a cyclic process or cycle. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.28

"Therefore, at the conclusion of a cycle, all the properties have the same value they had at the beginning." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.28

A reversible process can be reversed, leaving no change in the system or surroundings. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.28

"In reality, there are no truly reversible processes" DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.28

An irreversible process cannot return both the system and the surroundings to their original conditions. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.28

"Four of the most common causes of irreversibility are friction, unrestrained expansion of a fluid, heat transfer through a finite temperature difference, and mixing of two different substances." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.29
"An adiabatic process is one in which there is no heat transfer into or out of the system." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.29
"An isentropic process is one in which the entropy of the fluid remains constant." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.29

An isentropic process results if the process is reversible and adiabatic. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.29

In a polytropic process, a plot of Log P against Log V is a straight line, so PVⁿ is a constant. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.29

"A throttling process is defined as a process in which there is no change in enthalpy from state one to state two, h1 = h2; no work is done, W = 0; and the process is adiabatic, Q = 0." DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.29

An example of a throttling process is an ideal gas flowing through a valve in midposition. Pressure drops and velocity rises. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.29

Second law problems are treated much like first law problems, with an isolated, closed, or open system chosen by the energy crossing the boundary. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.75

  3. ∆h = 1178 - 1333

Answer: -155 Btu/lbm

Steam tables and quality

What are the enthalpy and entropy of steam at 90% quality and 400 psia? The tables give hf = 424.2, hfg = 780.4, sf = 0.6217, and sfg = 0.8630.

  1. h = hf + x(hfg) = 424.2 + (0.90)(780.4)
  2. s = sf + x(sfg) = 0.6217 + (0.9)(0.8630)

Answer: h = 1126.56 Btu/lbm and s = 1.3984 Btu/lbm-°R

First law on a steam turbine

Steam enters a turbine at 1349 Btu/lbm and leaves at 1100 Btu/lbm. Heat loss is 5 Btu/lbm. Inlet is 164 ft/sec and 6.5 ft above the discharge, and exit is 262 ft/sec. Find the work.

  1. Energy in: 1349 + 0.0084 + 0.5368 - 5 = 1344.54 Btu/lbm
  2. Energy out before work: 1100 + 1.37 = 1101.37 Btu/lbm
  3. w = 1344.54 - 1101.37

Answer: 243.17 Btu/lbm

Steam flows through a condenser at 2.0 x 10⁶ kg/hr, entering at h = 2574 kJ/kg and leaving at h = 125.8 kJ/kg. Cooling water goes from h = 75.6 to h = 104.9 kJ/kg. Find the cooling water flow.

Summary

  • Temperature is a measure of the molecular activity of a substance, and pressure is the force per unit area on its boundaries. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.12
  • Heat and work are energy in transit. Sensible heat changes temperature, and latent heat changes phase. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.25
  • Isolated, closed, and open systems differ in whether mass and energy cross the boundary. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.30
  • The First Law says energy can neither be created nor destroyed. The energy entering a component equals the energy leaving it at steady state. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.68
  • The Second Law sets the maximum efficiency of a system at the Carnot efficiency, η = (TH - TC)/TH. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.96
  • Efficiency is decreased by friction, heat losses, and cycle inefficiencies. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.96
  • The ideal gas law relates pressure, temperature, and volume during compression processes. DOE-HDBK-1012/1-92 HT-01 p.102
